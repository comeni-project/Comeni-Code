A read is a sample of a molecule, not the molecule

Because you have data
You sent tubes to a facility and got files back. The gap between those two things is the single idea on this page, and almost every confusing thing you will meet downstream is a consequence of it.

When you hand a tube to the sequencing facility, you are handing over millions of molecules, and what comes back is not those molecules — it is a few hundred letters read off some of them. Each read is one observation, drawn from a population you never see directly.

This is why coverage is spoken of as depth: sequencing the same library twice gives you two different samples of the same underlying pool. Two reads that look identical may come from one molecule read twice, or from two molecules that happen to share a sequence — and nothing in the file tells you which.

The consequence runs through everything downstream. A count is not a measurement of how much RNA was present; it is a count of how many times a sampling process happened to draw from that transcript. That is the whole reason differential expression needs statistics rather than subtraction.

What people usually think instead
That a FASTQ file is your sample — a complete, if messy, record of what was in the tube. It is easy to hold, and it survives right up until the day two replicates disagree and there is no obvious reason why.
See it on your own file
$ zcat sample_R1.fastq.gz | head -4
@A00489:52:HK3F2:1:1101:9425:1000 1:N:0:CGATGT GTTCAGGCCTTAGACATTGCCCTCCAGAGAATCTGGAAGGTCTGA + FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFF,FFFFFFF:FFFFFF,F:FFFFFFF
Four lines, one read. There are perhaps thirty million more in that file, and not one of them says which molecule it came from, or how many molecules there were.
